Why there is a bear.

The animal is Bruno the bear. My name is Bruno, and a bear is what you get when you say it out loud often enough. It walks rather than runs, which is roughly the pace I work at.

The brain in its head is the honest part. I know the organ I want to spend my life on. I do not know yet whether that means studying it, building for it, or opening it.

Most people are expected to have picked by now. I would rather stay accurate than sound decided, so the logo says both things at once: the direction is settled, the road is not.

About

How I got here.

I meant to become an anthropologist. Human imagination and intelligence fascinated me, so I went looking for them in politics, then in psychology, and ran into the same dissatisfaction each time. Those fields study human relationships well. But personality is contingent and culture-dependent, and I wanted something underneath it: a general model of how the brain works. That is what pointed me at neuroscience.

Medicine arrived from the other direction. During a hematology internship at Semmelweis University Hospital in Budapest, I got my first real look at a clinic from the inside. I left convinced there was still a lot to unlock in how diseases are detected, and in how the brain could be interfaced with technology. That is why I chose life sciences engineering over medical school. I wanted to understand both worlds deeply enough to connect them.

The question I keep coming back to is what shapes the way humans think, decide and change. It has taken me from EPFL's Laboratory of Cognitive Neuroscience, to neurotech at Roceso Technologies in Singapore, and on to a medical engineering exchange at KTH Stockholm and the Karolinska Institute.

Memory is not an abstract interest here. Several people in my family live with dementia, each of them differently, and work on stimulation, agency and spatial memory is the closest thing I have found to a way in.

Research

Things I have built.

Sense of Agency and Episodic Memory

Laidlaw research internship at EPFL's Laboratory of Cognitive Neuroscience. I built the online experiment testing whether the sense of agency, the feeling of controlling your own actions, strengthens episodic memory, and added confidence ratings to the recognition protocol.

Research report

Neurorehabilitation Robotics

R&D and clinical operations internship at Roceso Technologies, Singapore. Developing rehabilitation concepts for a hand-tracking device paired with a soft-robotic glove, grounded in agency and memory neuroscience, to help stroke patients recover.

Computer Vision on Psychiatric Art

Led the deconfounding pipeline in a 48-hour clinical AI pilot applying DINOv2 visual embeddings to 3,054 psychiatric patient artworks from Bern University Hospital. Text inscription explained roughly 10 times more visual variance than colour or figure content, and three expressive clusters mapped onto diagnoses.

EPFL Sustainable Innovation Challenge

Challenge Team Leader for Europe's first student-led sustainability forum of its kind. Over 11 months I led the challenge design and the selection of 8 finalist teams from 4 countries, plus a 7-member jury, closing with an international final at EPFL.

Hopfield Networks and Learning Rules

An empirical study of Hopfield networks as associative memory, comparing Hebbian and Storkey learning rules, storage capacity and robustness to noise. Awarded top recognition in EPFL's BIO-210 programming competition, a team of three among roughly 180 participants.

Population Simulation in C++

A dynamic ecosystem simulation in C++ with SFML. Animals and plants in a 2D toroidal world with reproduction, predation, gestation and perception, built with polymorphism, inheritance and design patterns.

Why the site looks like this.

My work is scientific, but design considerations shaped a great deal of how I think and how I approach science: what to make visible, what to leave out, how structure carries an argument before a single word is read. So I wanted this site built with those considerations in mind rather than assembled from a template.

That instinct is inherited. Kévés György, my grandmother's second husband and an architect, was the first person who made me want to design things at all. For a long time he was the reason I intended to become an architect.

My grandfather Czelnai Rudolf, a meteorologist, taught me to read the world as systems: pressure fields, evolutionary branches, the habit of drawing a phenomenon before claiming to understand it. The contour lines behind this section and the branching diagram below come directly from that.

The palette holds the two places I come from. Ochre, terracotta and Adriatic blue are the colours of Gorizia and the Friuli, my Italian roots, and the region of the Italian rationalists who ran the Bauhaus argument in their own accent. The green and the stone-beige belong to Celna, in Transylvania, the village the Czelnai name comes from. The shapes are Johannes Itten's Bauhaus grammar: triangle, square, circle, assigned here once and then used consistently.
